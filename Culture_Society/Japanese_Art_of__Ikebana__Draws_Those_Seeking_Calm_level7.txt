Arranging flowers might seem like a simple way to decorate a room. But for people who practice _ikebana_, it can also be a chance to slow down, appreciate nature and learn something about themselves.

_Ikebana_ is a Japanese art form that dates back more than 500 years. It involves arranging flowers, leaves and branches in a vase, paying attention to how each one grows and how they all look together. There is no single correct result: even with the same flowers, each person can create something different.

Hiroyuki Okada began taking _ikebana_ lessons about seven years ago, around the same time he started working at a flower shop. Today, he teaches _ikenobo_, the oldest of Japan's three major _ikebana_ schools.

Okada says people are drawn to _ikebana_ for joy, purpose or relief from the pressures of everyday life. For him, it is also a way to look inward.

"In arranging [flowers], you are dealing with the life that is there, the beauty, of course, but you find what's in your heart," he says. "You come face to face with yourself."

In one traditional _ikenobo_ style, flowers and branches are arranged as they might grow in nature, reaching toward sunlight or bending in the wind. A fading leaf beside a fresh bud can suggest different stages of life. Even the vase is part of the finished arrangement.

But there are other styles that are freer and look more like casual bouquets, which may feel easier for beginners.

People of all ages practice _ikebana_, from toddlers trying to copy their parents to devoted students who are still at it past the age of 100.

Mayu Shimizu, who has studied with Okada for about three years, compares the experience to yoga and meditation.

"I like this moment of nothingness when you are concentrating on the flowers before you," she says. "My mind feels clearer. I feel more at peace, and gentle."
