Do you use AI to help you shop? Many people do. A recent survey by business technology company CI&T found that three-quarters of Americans have used AI while shopping — often to compare brands or find lower prices.

But new research suggests that the recommendations you get may depend on what AI knows about you — and that, in many cases, AI will use that knowledge against you.

Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and Cisco Foundation AI created fictional users with different financial and demographic backgrounds. They then shared these users' profiles — and their fictional email inboxes — with AI agents running on a total of 13 different AI models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Alibaba.

Those fictional users then used the AI agents to make 325,000 inquiries about flights, health insurance and graduate school programs.

It was found that eight of the 13 models consistently recommended costlier options to wealthier users.

For example, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.8 recommended flights to wealthier users costing an average of {{prn:$198|one hundred and ninety eight dollars}} more. The gap was {{prn:$177|one hundred and seventy seven dollars}} for Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash, and {{prn:$107|one hundred and seven dollars}} for OpenAI's GPT-5.

Surprisingly, the difference persisted even when users specifically asked for the cheapest option. Gemini 2.5 Flash still recommended flights costing wealthier users an average of {{prn:$208|two hundred and eight dollars}} more, while GPT-5's gap was {{prn:$21|twenty one dollars}}.

The researchers suggest that words like "cheap" may be interpreted based on what the AI thinks a user can afford, rather than the lowest price. When the agents were given a specific budget, such as flights "under {{prn:$200|two hundred dollars}}," the disparity nearly disappeared.

It was also found that, even without being given access to users' financial profiles, the AI could infer their wealth just through access to their email inboxes.

The researchers call this problem "adversarial delegation" — because giving AI access to personal data makes it more useful in doing tasks for you, but also enables it to act against your interests.

They say the findings show that protecting users requires restricting not just what data AI can access, but how it is allowed to use it.
