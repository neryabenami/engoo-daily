ARTICLE

Everyone makes mistakes at work. But it's not always easy to admit when you've done something wrong.

One study published in _Frontiers in Organizational Psychology_ has found that women working in male-dominated workplaces see their mistakes far more negatively, and feel worse about them.

The research was based on survey data from close to 1,500 people working in the UK financial industry. While 81% of the survey participants were women, this is a very male-dominated industry.

Participants were asked how comfortable they were admitting to a mistake, how much they expected to be penalized for one, and if the mistake could be seen as a learning opportunity.

Then the researchers compared the survey answers with the participants' gender and the gender composition of their workplace.

On teams that were 75% or more male, women reported feeling less comfortable about mistakes, and were also less likely to view mistakes as learning opportunities compared to men.

In these male-dominated teams, on a scale from one to seven, women's level of comfort in admitting mistakes was {{prn:5.46|five point four six}}, while for men it was {{prn:5.94|five point nine four}}. Women also rated mistakes as chances to learn at {{prn:4.20|four point two zero}} out of seven, while among men it was {{prn:4.54|four point five four}}.

"Extremely male-heavy, high-pressure teams may foster a general climate of fear or perfectionism and a 'no mistakes' norm," said study author Grace Lordan from the London School of Economics.

However, there was very little difference in how much participants expected to be penalized for mistakes, no matter their gender or workplace composition.

The researchers said this may be because of the question's wording — penalization suggests something formal, so any expectations would be clearly written in company policies.

However, this doesn't mean workers don't have different levels of worry about less formal punishments, like having a mistake held against them by a coworker.
