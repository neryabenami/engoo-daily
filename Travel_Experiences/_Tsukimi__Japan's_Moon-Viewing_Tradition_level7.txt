When Galileo Galilei first saw the moon through a telescope in 1609, he described it as "a beautiful and delightful sight."

Of course, you don't need a telescope to see this beautiful and delightful sight!

And for some, looking at the moon is not just a pleasant activity, but an important tradition. Japan, for example, has a moon-viewing tradition known as _tsukimi_ or _otsukimi_ — and it goes back over 1,000 years!

_Tsukimi_ celebrations usually happen during the full moon in the fall months.

The tradition originated in China, where it's called the "Mid-Autumn Festival," and it began as a way for people to celebrate the moon goddess Chang'e. They would get together to admire the full moon, recite poetry and eat "mooncakes" with a sweet bean filling.

In Japan, a similar moon-viewing festival started becoming popular among aristocrats in the Heian period, which ran from the year 794 to 1185.

People would celebrate by taking boats out at night to look at the moon from the water, and they would recite poetry. Instead of mooncakes, they prepared _dango_, round rice dumplings that look like the full moon.

By the start of the Edo period, which was between 1603 and 1868, _tsukimi_ had become a commonly celebrated event among all Japanese people, not just among aristocrats.

In some places, it became associated with autumnal traditions, where people would give thanks for good harvests — and pray for a good one next year.

And as they still do today, people also made displays of _susuki_, or silver grass, believed to keep bad spirits away.

Originally, _tsukimi_ took place on the 13th night of the eighth month, which, according to the old lunar calendar, was when the moon was brightest.

And although Japan uses the Gregorian calendar today, the day of the festival is still decided based on the lunar calendar, and it takes place in September or October.
