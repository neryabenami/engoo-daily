Fewer hikers are climbing popular mountains in Japan's northern regions, according to new data analysis — and the reason might be bears.

IT company Uneri and _Kyodo News_ together analyzed three years of smartphone location data to see how many people came within 500 meters of the summits of Japan's _hyakumeizan_ or "100 famous mountains."

All of the data was taken with the smartphone users' consent.

Comparing the number of climbers between April and June this year with the same period two years ago, the analysts found that the number had declined across all 100 mountains by 18%.

However, in the Tohoku region of northeastern Honshu, there had been a decline of 32%. And in Hokkaido, Japan's northernmost island, the decline was 34%. Both of these regions have seen an increase in bear attacks and sightings in recent years.

And the areas where there have been more bear attacks saw the sharpest climbing declines.

Mount Hachimantai, which straddles the prefectures of Akita and {{prn:Iwate|Eewate}}, had the biggest decline at 46%. Those two prefectures had the greatest number of bear attacks in 2025: 59 in Akita and 39 in {{prn:Iwate|E-wah-teh}}.

Bear attacks have also been reported around Mount Kumotori, which straddles the prefectures of Tokyo, Saitama and Yamanashi. At that mountain, the number of climbers declined by 33%.

Kiyoshi Yamauchi, an expert in bear ecology at {{prn:Iwate|Eewate}} University, told _Kyodo News_ that a series of bear attacks in recent months may have made hikers nervous.

However, he said that many bear attacks actually happen in areas near homes. Bears in the mountains are more fearful of people and more likely to stay away from them, he explained.

But he added that hikers should still be careful not to surprise bears. He recommended carrying bear bells and whistles and staying on proper trails.
